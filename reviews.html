— Myra Puri

There can be drills for better pointed feet. But can expressions be taught to improve your stage presence and performance quality?

Faces are the most prominent object in most performances. Eyes, micro-expressions, the question of whether the emotion was of happiness or sadness. Processing all of it takes ability, and that ability has to come from somewhere. Dance happens in the mind, not in the body. You must not have loved and lost but still be able to express the emotions of love, longing and pain. One must stay present in what is being said and the audience should be able to connect with the character. You can also use your breath to amplify the emotional narrative rather than relying solely on the face.

You are always told dance to express and not impress. But the faces watching you sometimes make that even harder. Where your eyes are directed matters. Technically correct movement is only part of what makes a performance memorable. What matters most is that you are able to convey your intention and story through your movements and expressions.

When someone watches contemporary, they are looking beyond clean technique. They are watching how the dancer moves, uses the eyes, connects to the music, and communicates through the entire body.

I was 4. The lights flash, blinding me, as I look out into the abyss that is the auditorium and I hear dozens of people screaming my name. In that moment, I knew I wanted to be a dancer.

Every time I stepped on that stage since then, I got a surge of energy, emotion, and confidence. The thought that these people have come to see me perform, the thought that they’ve made time out of their busy schedules for watching my dance, the thought that they cheer every time I do something, it was all so beautiful and overwhelming at the same time.

But I realised something. With each performance, with each step on the stage, the overwhelming feeling kept reducing. All it was was exciting, and the amount of energy I got on the stage, was worth all the toiling and the hard work put in.

And suddenly, even in my personal life, I started to talk to more people, make conversations and be more confident in myself. Those performances helped me put myself more out there in real life, and people around me started to appreciate my self love and confidence, all because of dance.

If I never had the confidence to dance in front of hundreds of people, I would’ve never thought to talk to people and become the extrovert that I am.

Then came Ms. Nansi.

Ever since I started contemporary, and I started doing movements which flowed and focused on flexibility I started to become much more relaxed and calm in my dance and myself. Ms Nansi always pushes me to my full potential, and more.
“Sanvika, point your toes!”
“Sanvika, back straight!”
“Sanvika, tailbone in!”
We started to do so many more performances and shows, and each time, the stage felt like a friend instead of an inanimate object.

At Performerz, we’re allowed to express ourselves however we want, and that pushes us forward in our personal lives too, no matter what’s going on behind the curtains.

There is nothing better than the moment of applause from several people as you strike your last pose. And that moment? It’s the reason that dance is my favourite thing ever.

— Written by Sanvika

Ever since I can remember, I have experienced stage fright. On many occasions, I have even declined opportunities to perform just to avoid that feeling. It often made me feel underprepared and underconfident, which would then reflect in my performances.

As someone who has grown up doing dance and taking part in different kinds of artistic performances, stage fear is something I have constantly had to deal with. What helped me the most, however, was understanding that stage fear is not an isolated experience. Every artist, whether amateur or experienced, feels some form of fear right before getting on stage. In fact, it is a universal experience and an almost unavoidable part of being a performer.

People often assume that it is the crowd that makes someone anxious before getting on stage. However, I have found that regardless of how many people are sitting in front of you, the anxiety can still be there. It is actually the thoughts running through your mind that often control the situation.

For me, these thoughts are often: “What if I forget my steps? What if I go offbeat? What if I fall?” and so on. Adequate training and practice, positive affirmations, and breathing exercises can definitely help reduce the intensity of these thoughts. However, completely getting rid of stage fear is often not possible.

Over time, I have learned to look at stage fear differently. Instead of seeing it only as something negative, I have started to see it as a driving force and a source of motivation before a performance. When we reframe anxiety as excitement, our perception of the situation can change. The same feeling that once made us nervous can become the energy that helps us perform.

So, to all my fellow dancers and artists who struggle with stage fear, I would say, own the feeling instead of fighting it. Let the nervousness remind you that you care about what you are about to do, and use that energy to fuel your performance.

At the end of the day, the whole point of dancing is to express yourself, share your craft, and keep getting better at it. When difficult thoughts come in and make you question your abilities, try to shift your focus away from everything that could go wrong and back towards the art itself.

You do not have to stop being afraid to step onto the stage. Sometimes, you just have to step onto it with the fear—and dance anyway.

— By Minakshi

I still remember being a kid, sitting in front of the television, watching reality dance shows. I would watch the dancers move across the stage, sometimes falling, sometimes jumping, sometimes doing movements that looked almost impossible. But what fascinated me most was how different each dancer looked. It felt like the dance belonged to them.

I remember thinking, How can the same dance form look so different on every person?

Then the judges would say, “That was contemporary.”

I didn’t really understand what that meant at the time. When I looked up the word, I found that it meant “what is happening right now.” I couldn’t quite understand how that connected to the dance I had just watched, but I knew I wanted to learn it.

Fast-forward to when I was 26, and I finally found Performerz Academy.

Before that, I had learnt choreography in semi-classical, Bharatanatyam, freestyle, and other dance forms. For me, dancing had always been about hitting the right step on the right beat. The music decided the beat, and someone else had already decided the steps.

So, when I walked into my first contemporary class, I thought I knew what dancing was supposed to feel like. I was wrong.

I still remember looking at my teacher and thinking, Why does that movement look so beautiful on them and so awkward on me?

The floor movements felt strange. The jumps felt forced. Even the simplest movements made me conscious of how I looked. But I kept going.

And somewhere along the way, something started to change.

I began to feel more comfortable with the basic movements my body could make. Slowly, the things that had once felt weird started to feel natural. I began to understand that I didn’t always have to tell my body how to move. Sometimes, I could let my body tell me what to feel.

That was when I started to understand what contemporary really meant to me. It was about being present. It was about listening to my body instead of constantly correcting it. It was about moving without worrying whether every movement looked perfect. And it was about discovering that I could express something even when I didn’t have the words for it.

Contemporary dance taught me that uncertainty doesn’t always need to be solved. There are things I cannot say, but I can move them.

Maybe that is what makes contemporary so personal. The movement is not just about the dancer. It is also about what the dancer is feeling in that moment. And maybe, just like the dancers I watched as a kid, I am still learning how to make the dance my own.

Contemporary dance changed my relationship with being seen. Instead of constantly asking myself, “How do I look?” I started asking, “What do I want to express?”

And that, to me, is what contemporary means. It is not just about learning how to move. It is about learning how to feel, how to express, and how to be present.

— Written by Anushka Pai
